Can You Sue for Nerve Damage in a Car Accident?

Nerve injuries are real, hard to see, and routinely undervalued. Here is how they get proven.

Nerve injuries occupy a frustrating middle ground in car accident claims. The symptoms are often severe and genuinely disabling, but they do not show up on the kind of imaging that makes a broken bone obvious. That gap between how much an injury affects your life and how easily it can be demonstrated is exactly where insurers apply pressure.

The short answer to the question in the title is yes: nerve damage is a compensable injury, and if someone else's negligence caused your crash you can generally pursue a claim for it. The longer and more useful answer is about proof.

What Nerve Damage After a Crash Actually Looks Like

Nerve injuries from motor vehicle collisions generally fall into a few categories. Compression or impingement happens when a herniated or bulging disc presses on a nerve root, commonly in the neck or lower back — radiating pain down an arm or leg is the classic presentation. Stretch and traction injuries occur when the body is violently thrown, with brachial plexus injuries in the shoulder and neck being a well-known example. Lacerations from broken glass or metal can sever nerves directly. Crush injuries can damage nerves alongside the surrounding tissue.

Symptoms people report include numbness or a persistent pins-and-needles sensation, burning or electric-shock pain, muscle weakness, loss of grip strength, coordination difficulty, and sensitivity to touch or temperature. Some symptoms surface immediately; others develop over days or weeks as swelling and inflammation progress, which is one reason we always suggest a medical evaluation even when you feel functional right after a crash.

Why These Claims Get Disputed

Three arguments come up repeatedly, and understanding them explains most of what a good lawyer does on a nerve case.

"The symptoms are subjective." Numbness and burning pain cannot be photographed. If the file contains only your description of how you feel, an adjuster will treat it as unverifiable.

"It is degenerative, not traumatic." Disc degeneration is extremely common and increases with age, and it frequently appears on imaging in people with no symptoms at all. Insurers seize on any degenerative finding to argue your problem predates the crash. The counterargument is usually not that your spine was pristine, but that you were asymptomatic and functional before the collision and were not afterward — which is why records of your pre-crash health matter.

"There is a gap in treatment." If you waited weeks to seek care, or stopped attending appointments, that gap will be presented as evidence you were not really injured. This is the most avoidable of the three.

The Evidence That Moves These Cases

What tends to separate a nerve claim that resolves well from one that stalls is objective testing.

Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies measure electrical activity in muscles and how quickly signals travel along nerves. These are among the few tests that can objectively document nerve dysfunction, and results are difficult to dismiss as subjective complaint.

MRI can reveal structural causes such as disc herniation compressing a nerve root, which links a mechanism to your symptoms.

A documented neurological examination — reflex testing, strength grading, sensory mapping — creates a record from a physician rather than from you.

Consistent treatment records across time show a real, ongoing problem rather than a brief complaint.

Testimony from people who know you is more valuable than most people expect. A supervisor describing dropped tools or a spouse describing tasks you can no longer manage is concrete in a way that "I have pain" is not.

Timing matters too. Seeking evaluation promptly and following through on referrals does two things at once: it improves your medical outcome and it removes the insurer's easiest argument.

What a Nerve Damage Claim May Cover

Damages depend on your state's law and the facts of your case, but nerve injury claims commonly involve past and future medical costs — including EMG and imaging, specialist care, physical therapy, pain management, medication, and in some cases surgery such as a discectomy or nerve decompression. They may also involve lost wages, and where the injury is permanent, reduced future earning capacity. That last category matters enormously for people whose work depends on grip strength, fine motor control, or standing for long periods.

Non-economic damages for pain, loss of enjoyment of life, and emotional distress are also typically available. Chronic neuropathic pain is exhausting in a way that is hard to convey on paper, which is another reason a contemporaneous symptom journal is worth keeping.

If your nerve injury turns out to be permanent, the valuation analysis changes substantially — we covered that in more detail in our guide on permanent disability after a car accident.

Do Not Settle Before You Know What You Have

This is the point we would emphasize above all others. Nerve injuries are notoriously unpredictable. Some resolve substantially within months as inflammation subsides. Others plateau with permanent deficits. Some worsen.

Insurers frequently offer early settlements on nerve claims precisely because the eventual outcome is unknown and an early number is usually cheaper than the real one. Once you sign a release, the claim is closed — if you need surgery a year later, that is your expense.

The general principle is to avoid settling until you reach maximum medical improvement, the point where your treating physicians can say what is permanent. That may take many months, which is inconvenient when bills are arriving, but settling early on an unresolved nerve injury is a decision that cannot be undone.

Deadlines and Getting Help

Every state sets a filing deadline for injury lawsuits, and claims against government entities often carry much shorter notice requirements. Because nerve symptoms can take time to fully declare themselves, people sometimes assume the clock has not started — generally it starts at the accident, not at diagnosis, though some states apply a discovery rule in limited circumstances. Our overview of personal injury statutes of limitations explains how these deadlines work and why you should confirm yours locally.

Because nerve claims live or die on medical evidence, they are among the case types where representation tends to add the most value: coordinating the right specialists, obtaining EMG and imaging, retaining experts who can explain causation, and countering the degenerative-condition argument with a documented before-and-after picture.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you sue for nerve damage caused by a car accident?

Generally yes. If another party's negligence caused the crash and the crash caused your nerve injury, nerve damage is a compensable injury like any other. The practical challenge is usually proving the injury and its cause with objective medical evidence rather than establishing the legal right to sue.

How is nerve damage from a car accident diagnosed?

Diagnosis typically combines a clinical neurological examination with testing. Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies measure how well nerves and muscles are functioning, while MRI or CT imaging can identify structural causes such as a herniated disc compressing a nerve root. Which tests are appropriate depends on your symptoms.

Why do insurance companies dispute nerve damage claims?

Nerve injuries often do not appear on a standard X-ray and symptoms like numbness, tingling, and burning pain are subjective. Insurers commonly argue the symptoms are exaggerated, stem from a pre-existing degenerative condition, or are unrelated to the crash — which is why objective testing and consistent treatment records matter so much.

What to Do in the First 72 Hours After a Car Accident

What to do at the scene, in the first day, and before the week is out.

Almost nobody is thinking clearly right after a crash. You are shaken, possibly hurt, and suddenly responsible for a list of tasks nobody prepared you for. The decisions made in the first few days tend to shape how the rest of the claim goes, and the most damaging mistakes are rarely dramatic — they are small omissions that seem reasonable at the time.

Here is what we would want a friend to know, in the order it usually matters. Your health comes first in every case; the documentation advice below is secondary to getting care.

At the Scene: The First Hour

Check for injuries and call 911. Report injuries even if they seem minor. In many places a police response is required above a certain damage threshold, and a police report becomes a foundational document later.

Get to safety. If the vehicles are drivable and you are on a live roadway, moving to the shoulder is usually safer than staying in traffic. If anyone is seriously hurt, do not move them unless there is an immediate danger such as fire.

Exchange information. You need the other driver's name, contact details, insurance company and policy number, license plate, and vehicle description. Photographing their insurance card and license is faster and more accurate than writing it down.

Photograph more than you think you need. Wide shots showing the position of both vehicles and the overall scene, close-ups of all damage on both cars, skid marks, debris, traffic signals and signs, road conditions, weather, and any visible injuries. Photos are free and memory is unreliable.

Look for witnesses. Independent witnesses are disproportionately valuable because they have no stake in the outcome. Get names and phone numbers before people leave — they rarely stay long, and the police report may not capture everyone.

Be careful what you say. Apologizing is a human reflex, but "I'm so sorry" can be characterized later as an admission of fault. Stick to facts with the police and the other driver. It is fine to say you are not sure what happened, because at that moment you probably are not.

Before You Go to Bed: Hours 1 Through 12

Get medically evaluated, even if you feel okay. This is the single most common regret we hear about. Adrenaline suppresses pain, and concussions, soft tissue injuries, and internal bleeding can all present hours or days later. An emergency department or urgent care visit the same day creates a record tying your injuries to the crash. Without it, an insurer will argue that whatever hurts must have come from something else.

Report the accident to your own insurance company. Nearly every policy requires prompt notice, and delay can jeopardize your own coverage — including benefits you may be entitled to regardless of fault, such as personal injury protection or medical payments coverage in states that have them.

Write down what happened while it is fresh. Direction of travel, speeds, light color, weather, what you saw and heard, what was said afterward. Memory degrades quickly and it degrades in ways that feel confident. A contemporaneous note is far more persuasive than a recollection months later.

Day Two: Documentation and Follow-Through

Follow the treatment plan you were given. If you were told to see your primary doctor, follow up with a specialist, or start physical therapy, do it and do it on schedule. Gaps in treatment are the most common argument insurers use to devalue an injury claim — the reasoning being that if you truly hurt, you would have kept going to the doctor.

Start a folder and keep everything. Medical bills, discharge paperwork, prescription receipts, the police report number, repair estimates, rental car receipts, and any correspondence from either insurer.

Keep a short daily symptom log. A few lines a day: pain level, what you could not do, sleep quality, work missed. This sounds tedious, and it is the evidence people most wish they had when it comes time to describe how an injury actually affected their life. Vague testimony about pain is much weaker than a dated record.

Request the police report once it becomes available, and read it. Reports do contain errors — wrong direction of travel, misidentified vehicles, incorrect insurance details. Correcting an error is far easier early than after it has been relied on for months.

Day Three: Handling the Insurance Call

By now the other driver's insurer has probably called. This call is where a good claim gets quietly damaged.

The adjuster will be friendly and will likely ask for two things: a recorded statement and a signed medical authorization. Both deserve caution.

You are generally obligated to cooperate with your own insurance company under the terms of your policy. You are typically not obligated to give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurer. Recorded statements are taken early precisely because you do not yet know the extent of your injuries — and "I'm doing okay" said on day three becomes a problem when a herniated disc surfaces in week six.

Broad medical authorizations are a related issue. A blanket release can give an insurer access to your entire medical history, which they will mine for pre-existing conditions to blame your symptoms on. Providing records relevant to this injury is normal; opening your whole file usually is not necessary.

It is perfectly acceptable to say you are still being treated, you are not prepared to give a statement, and you will follow up. Politeness costs nothing and concedes nothing.

What Not to Do This Week

  • Do not post about the accident on social media. Insurers do look. A photo of you at a family barbecue, captioned cheerfully, will be offered as evidence you are not really hurt — context be damned. Consider pausing posting entirely while a claim is open.
  • Do not accept a quick settlement. Early offers arrive before anyone knows what your treatment will cost. Once you sign a release, the claim is closed permanently, even if you need surgery two months later.
  • Do not repair or dispose of your vehicle before it has been documented, and preserve any dashcam footage immediately. The vehicle itself is evidence.
  • Do not exaggerate. Overstating symptoms is the fastest way to destroy your credibility on everything else, including the parts that are true.
  • Do not ignore deadlines. Every state sets a filing deadline, and claims involving government entities often carry much shorter notice requirements. See our guide on statutes of limitations.

When to Call a Lawyer

Not every fender bender needs an attorney. But we would make the call sooner rather than later if any of these apply: you were meaningfully injured, someone was hospitalized or died, fault is disputed, the other driver was uninsured, a commercial truck or government vehicle was involved, the insurer has denied the claim, or you have simply been asked for a recorded statement and feel out of your depth.

Early involvement matters for reasons that are not obvious. Surveillance video is often overwritten within days or weeks. Commercial vehicles carry electronic data that can be lost on a normal retention schedule. Witnesses move and phone numbers stop working. A lawyer can send preservation letters to stop that clock — but only if they are retained while the evidence still exists.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I go to the doctor if I feel fine after a car accident?

Getting evaluated is generally wise. Adrenaline can mask pain for hours or days, and some serious injuries — including concussions and internal injuries — do not produce obvious symptoms immediately. A prompt evaluation also creates a medical record connecting any injuries to the crash.

Do I have to give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance company?

You are generally required to cooperate with your own insurer under your policy, but you are typically not obligated to give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance company. Many people decline until they have spoken with an attorney.

How soon should I contact a lawyer after a car accident?

There is no requirement to hire one at all, but earlier is generally better if you were injured, because evidence like video footage and vehicle data can disappear quickly. Consultations are typically free, so an early conversation costs you nothing but time.

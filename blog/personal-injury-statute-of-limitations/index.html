Personal Injury Statute of Limitations by State: What You Need to Know

Miss this deadline and it usually does not matter how strong your case was.

Of everything on this site, this is the topic where getting it wrong has the most severe consequences. A statute of limitations is a hard deadline. File one day late and the defendant will move to dismiss, and in most circumstances the court will grant it — no matter how clear the liability, how serious the injuries, or how sympathetic the facts.

We want to be straightforward about how we are handling this article, because it differs from how most sites treat it.

Why We Are Not Publishing a 50-State Chart

You will find many pages offering a tidy table of all fifty states with a number next to each one. We decided against publishing one, and the reasoning is worth explaining.

These deadlines change. Several states have amended their personal injury limitation periods in recent years, and charts across the web are frequently out of date. Worse, a single deadline per state is misleading on its face, because the applicable period depends on the type of claim: medical malpractice, wrongful death, product liability, claims against government entities, and ordinary negligence often carry different deadlines within the same state.

A stale or oversimplified number in a chart is not a harmless error here. Someone could read it, believe they have time, and lose their claim permanently. We would rather explain how the rules work and tell you to verify the specific number than hand you a figure we cannot guarantee is current for your situation.

What we can tell you generally: most states set the ordinary personal injury deadline somewhere in the range of two to three years from the date of injury, with outliers shorter and longer. Treat that as orientation, not as your deadline.

When the Clock Usually Starts

In a typical car accident case, the clock starts on the date of the crash. That is the default and it applies to most straightforward injury claims.

The main exception is the discovery rule, which applies in states and circumstances where an injury could not reasonably have been discovered at the time it occurred. It arises most often in medical malpractice — a surgical instrument left in the body, for instance, or a misdiagnosis that only becomes apparent much later — and in toxic exposure cases where illness develops years afterward. Where it applies, the clock may start when the injury was discovered or reasonably should have been discovered.

The discovery rule is narrower than people hope. It generally will not extend the deadline simply because your symptoms worsened over time or because you did not realize how serious an injury was. Many states also impose a statute of repose, an outer limit that cuts off claims after a fixed period regardless of discovery.

The Deadlines That Are Much Shorter Than You Expect

This is the section we would most want someone to read.

Claims against government entities — a city bus, a county vehicle, a state agency, a public hospital, a hazardous condition on public property — frequently require a formal notice of claim filed long before any lawsuit, often within a matter of months rather than years. Missing that notice deadline can bar the claim even though the general statute of limitations has years to run. If any government entity might be involved in your accident, this is an urgent question, not a later one.

Medical malpractice commonly carries its own deadline and frequently additional procedural prerequisites, such as an affidavit or certificate of merit from a qualified medical expert filed with or before the complaint.

Wrongful death claims typically run from the date of death rather than the date of injury, and are often governed by a separate statute from ordinary personal injury.

Dram shop claims, product liability, and claims against certain regulated entities may each carry distinct periods.

Circumstances That Can Pause the Clock

Tolling refers to circumstances that pause the running of the limitations period. The most common are:

  • Minors. Many states toll the deadline for injured children, often until they reach the age of majority — though notice requirements against government entities may not be tolled the same way.
  • Legal incapacity. Where an injured person is incapacitated and unable to manage their affairs, the period may be tolled.
  • Defendant absence or concealment. If a defendant leaves the state or fraudulently conceals their role, the clock may pause.

Every one of these has its own conditions and limits, and none should be relied on without confirmation from a lawyer in your state. Assuming tolling applies and being wrong produces the same outcome as missing the deadline outright.

Why Waiting Costs You Even When the Deadline Is Far Off

People sometimes read "two years" and conclude there is no rush. In practice, the practical deadlines run much faster than the legal one.

Surveillance and dashcam video is commonly overwritten within days or weeks. Commercial vehicle data can be lost when the truck returns to service. Physical evidence at a scene changes; skid marks fade and roadwork happens. Witnesses move, change numbers, and forget details — and the detail they forget is usually the one that mattered.

There is also a claim-quality reason. Insurance adjusters treat delay as a signal. A claim reported promptly, with treatment beginning immediately and continuing consistently, is simply harder to attack than one that surfaces eighteen months later.

How to Find Your Actual Deadline

Three practical steps:

  1. Talk to a personal injury lawyer licensed in your state. Consultations are typically free, and identifying the applicable deadline is one of the first things they will do. This is the only reliable answer.
  2. Tell them every entity that might be involved. Government vehicles, public property, a commercial employer, a medical provider — each can bring a different and shorter deadline into play.
  3. Do it now rather than later. If you are already close to a deadline, some firms will decline the case simply because there is not enough time left to prepare it properly. Time is the one asset in a claim that cannot be recovered.

If you take one thing from this article, let it be this: the deadline that applies to you is a specific, knowable fact, and finding out costs nothing. Guessing at it, or trusting a chart of uncertain vintage, risks everything.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a statute of limitations in a personal injury case?

It is a law setting the maximum time after an injury within which you can file a lawsuit. If you file after the deadline, the defendant can move to dismiss the case, and courts generally grant that motion regardless of how strong the underlying claim is.

How long is the personal injury statute of limitations in most states?

Most states fall in the range of two to three years from the date of injury, but there are states on both sides of that range, and several have changed their deadlines in recent years. Because the consequence of getting it wrong is losing the claim entirely, confirm your state's current deadline with a licensed attorney rather than relying on a general figure.

Can the deadline to file be extended?

Sometimes. Doctrines such as the discovery rule, tolling for minors or incapacity, and defendant absence can extend deadlines in specific circumstances. These are narrow exceptions with their own rules, and they should never be assumed to apply without legal advice.

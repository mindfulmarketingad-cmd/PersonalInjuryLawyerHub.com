What Happens When a Trucking Company Denies Fault After a Truck Accident?

A denial is a negotiating position, not a verdict. Here is what determines whether it holds.

If a trucking company or its insurer has told you the crash was not their driver's fault, the first thing worth understanding is that this is routine. Commercial carriers and their insurers often dispute liability as an opening position, sometimes within hours of the collision and long before any real investigation has occurred. A denial is the start of a negotiation, not a determination.

What follows is what actually happens next, and why the first few weeks matter more in truck cases than in almost any other kind of accident claim.

Why Carriers Deny Fault So Quickly

The economics explain the behavior. Commercial trucks are required to carry far higher liability coverage than passenger vehicles, and crashes involving heavy vehicles tend to produce severe injuries. The exposure is large enough that carriers and their insurers treat serious collisions as litigation from the outset.

Many carriers have rapid response protocols. Investigators and defense counsel can be dispatched to a scene the same day, photographing conditions, interviewing the driver, and securing the vehicle before an injured person has left the hospital. This is not sinister — it is competent risk management — but it means the other side frequently has a substantial evidentiary head start.

Denials commonly take a few forms: that the other motorist caused the collision, that a mechanical failure was an unforeseeable event outside the carrier's control, that a third party such as a maintenance contractor or cargo loader is responsible, or that the driver was an independent contractor rather than an employee.

The Evidence That Disappears First

This is the most important practical section of this article, because the window is short.

Electronic logging device (ELD) data records driving hours and duty status, which bears directly on fatigue and hours-of-service compliance. Federal recordkeeping requirements are finite, and data outside the required retention period may not be kept.

Engine control module and telematics data can capture speed, braking, throttle position, and sudden deceleration events around the crash. This data can be overwritten as the vehicle continues in service, and it is often lost simply because the truck went back on the road.

Dashcam and facility video is frequently on a short overwrite cycle — days or weeks in many systems. The same applies to traffic and business surveillance cameras near the scene.

Driver qualification files, training records, drug and alcohol testing records, and maintenance and inspection logs are all governed by retention schedules, and once a schedule lapses, routine destruction is not necessarily improper.

This is why a preservation letter — often called a spoliation letter — going out early matters so much. It formally notifies the carrier that specific categories of evidence are relevant to a claim and must be retained. If evidence is destroyed after such notice, courts have remedies available, which can include adverse inference instructions. Without that notice, ordinary destruction may carry no consequence at all.

More Than One Party May Be Responsible

Truck cases differ from car cases in that liability is often distributed. Depending on the facts, the responsible parties can include the driver, the motor carrier that employed or contracted with them, the owner of the tractor or trailer where they differ, a maintenance provider, the company that loaded or secured the cargo, a broker or shipper, or a manufacturer if a component failed.

This matters for a concrete reason: each of those parties may carry separate insurance. A denial from one carrier does not resolve the claim if another party's negligence contributed. Identifying every potentially responsible entity is a substantial part of the early work in a truck case, and it is one reason these claims benefit from counsel experienced specifically with commercial vehicles rather than general car accident practice.

The Independent Contractor Argument

One denial deserves individual attention because it appears so often: the claim that the driver was an independent contractor, not an employee, and that the carrier therefore is not responsible for their conduct.

Labels in a contract are not automatically decisive. Courts examine the actual relationship — the degree of control the carrier exercised over the driver's work, whose equipment was used, how the driver was paid and dispatched, and whether the truck displayed the carrier's operating authority. Federal motor carrier regulations also address the responsibility of carriers for vehicles operating under their authority. This is a fact-intensive question, and an early contractual label is not the end of the analysis.

What Happens After the Denial

In practice, a denied truck claim typically proceeds along these lines. Evidence is preserved and gathered — the police report, scene photographs, witness statements, and any available video. Records are requested from the carrier, and when they are not produced voluntarily, they are pursued through formal discovery once suit is filed.

Experts frequently become involved: accident reconstruction specialists who can analyze physical evidence and vehicle data, and trucking safety experts who can evaluate compliance with federal regulations covering hours of service, maintenance, inspection, and driver qualification. Regulatory violations can be powerful evidence, because they establish a standard the carrier was required to meet and did not.

Depositions of the driver, dispatchers, safety directors, and maintenance personnel often produce the admissions that shift a case. Many claims that began with a firm denial resolve once discovery makes the underlying facts clear.

What You Should Do Now

  • Keep treating and follow your medical plan. Gaps in care are used against injury claims regardless of how strong liability is.
  • Preserve everything you have — photographs, the police report number, the truck's DOT number if you captured it, witness contacts, and your own written account while memory is fresh.
  • Decline recorded statements to the carrier's insurer until you have advice. Their investigator is working for the other side.
  • Do not accept an early offer. Serious injuries take time to evaluate, and early offers precede that evaluation.
  • Act quickly on evidence. The legal deadline to file suit may be years away, but the practical deadline to preserve ELD, telematics, and video data can be measured in weeks.

A denial can feel like the end of the road, particularly when it arrives with confident language from a company with obvious resources. It is worth remembering that the party issuing the denial is the party that would have to pay, and that they are stating a position rather than reporting a finding.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do if a trucking company denies responsibility for the crash?

A denial early in the process is common and is not the final word. The important step is preserving evidence quickly — electronic logging device data, telematics, dashcam footage, and maintenance records can be overwritten or discarded on routine retention schedules, so a spoliation or preservation letter should go out as soon as possible.

Can more than one party be liable in a truck accident?

Yes, and this is a key difference from ordinary car accidents. Depending on the facts, potentially responsible parties can include the driver, the motor carrier, the company that owned or leased the trailer, a maintenance contractor, a cargo loader, or a parts manufacturer. Identifying all of them matters because each may carry separate insurance.

How long do I have to file a truck accident lawsuit?

That depends on your state's statute of limitations, and shorter notice deadlines can apply if a government entity is involved. Because critical trucking evidence can be lost within weeks regardless of the filing deadline, acting quickly matters even when the legal deadline is years away.
